# Business Analysis

## Supplier-Side Planning Concept

Agri-AI is built around a supplier-side planning problem:
what should be produced, when, and in what quantity before supply exists.

This is different from warehouse or post-harvest software.
The system is meant to influence production and commitment decisions upstream.

## How It Differs From Normal ERP Or Warehouse Systems

Traditional ERP, inventory, and produce operations tools typically handle:

- ordering
- fulfillment
- traceability
- inventory control
- post-harvest operations

Agri-AI should focus earlier than that.

Its job is to help an operator translate buyer demand into grower commitments before the season locks.

## Indiana / Midwest Pilot Angle

Indiana is a strong proving ground for the first pilot because:

- there is meaningful specialty-crop and local food activity
- Purdue and local food networks create discovery pathways
- the region is practical enough to test operator workflows without pretending the market is infinite

The Midwest is not the entire story.
The initial market should be treated as a specialty-crop coordination wedge, not a broad agriculture claim.

## Target Customer Wedge

The most credible first customer is not the independent farm.

Best wedge:

- regional food hub
- multi-grower aggregator
- produce distributor with a local grower program
- operator coordinating recurring wholesale or institutional demand

Why:

- the pain is operationally visible
- the downside is measurable
- the operator already sits between buyers and growers
- they can become the distribution channel for grower-side adoption later

## MVP Framing

The MVP should be a planning layer, not a full platform.

It should answer:

- where demand exceeds expected supply
- where supply exceeds likely demand
- which weeks are high risk
- what operator action should happen next

Manual support is acceptable in early pilots.
The venture does not need heavy AI or full automation to prove value.

## Pricing Direction

Early pricing should be grounded in seasonal value, not fantasy enterprise spend.

Practical direction:

- pilot fee per season
- optional analytics or support fee
- annual contract only after pilot proof

Pricing range:

- pilot: $5,000 to $15,000
- annual contract: $12,000 to $40,000

## Pricing Rationale

These numbers are not arbitrary. They need to be anchored to the operator's actual cost of supply failure.

A mid-size operator managing $2M–$4M in annual specialty crop GMV typically experiences:

- 2–5 emergency buy events per season at a 15–25% price premium over planned cost
- 5–10% of order lines unfilled or substituted in high-demand weeks
- direct margin loss from overproduction and spoilage in low-demand weeks

Conservative estimate of annual supply mismatch cost for a $2M GMV operator:

- emergency buys: $30,000–$80,000 in excess cost
- unfilled line revenue loss: $50,000–$150,000 in forgone revenue
- total visible pain: $80,000–$230,000 per year

At a $10,000 pilot fee, the ask is roughly 4–12% of the pain the pilot is attempting to address.
That is a reasonable ratio if the operator can see the cost clearly.

The discovery call should surface these numbers explicitly, not assume the operator has already calculated them.
If the operator cannot articulate their supply failure cost, the pilot pitch will not close.

## The Coordinator Objection

The most common implicit objection: we could just hire a better coordinator or use more spreadsheets.

This must be addressed directly.

A full-time supply coordinator costs $45,000–$70,000 per year in salary plus overhead.

Agri-AI at $12,000–$40,000 per year is not cheaper than a coordinator.
It is a leverage tool for the coordinator who already exists.

The right framing:

- your coordinator spends most of their time assembling the picture manually, not acting on it
- Agri-AI compresses the assembly time and surfaces the risk earlier
- the coordinator's judgment and grower relationships remain essential
- the output is a coordinator who acts earlier and with better information, not an automated replacement

If the operator has no coordinator and the founder becomes the de facto planning support, that is a services business, not a software business.
Watch for this pattern in early pilots and design the transition to software-led delivery deliberately.

## GTM Direction

Start with a narrow, operator-first motion.

1. identify buyer-facing aggregators with recurring shortage pain
2. win paid pilot access to historical order and grower data
3. deliver weekly planning output manually or semi-manually
4. prove impact on fill rate, emergency buys, or overage reduction
5. convert into annual planning workflow

## Risks

- operators may not pay if planning pain is not tied to measurable cost
- data may be messy or incomplete
- food hubs and local operators can be budget constrained
- many existing tools already own adjacent workflow
- product can bloat if it tries to replace ERP, logistics, and buyer sourcing all at once

## What Must Be Validated

1. real willingness to pay from the operator, not just interest
2. ability to get usable order and grower data quickly
3. repeatable weekly output that changes decisions
4. a clear path from pilot service to durable software workflow

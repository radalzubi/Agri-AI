# Market Reality

## Core Market Truth

This is not a broad agriculture software opportunity in its first phase.
It is a specialty-crop supply coordination opportunity targeting a specific operator type: buyer-facing aggregators and food hubs managing recurring grower networks.

## Current State

Growers and operators already plan, but they do so in fragmented ways.

Planning is spread across:

- spreadsheets
- email
- phone calls
- buyer memory
- grower assumptions

The problem is not that nobody plans.
The problem is that nobody coordinates the full picture well before the season starts.

## Indiana and Midwest Market Sizing

Rough estimates based on USDA Local Food research and NAFSN data. These are directional, not audited.

- Indiana has approximately 15–20 active food hubs and regional produce aggregators
- The Midwest (IN, IL, OH, MI, WI) has approximately 100–130 food hubs total
- Median food hub annual sales are in the range of $1M–$3M based on USDA data
- Larger food hubs operating above $5M GMV are a minority but represent better commercial targets

At $10,000–$20,000 annual contract value per operator:

- Indiana alone: $150K–$300K ARR at full penetration
- Midwest five-state: $1M–$2.5M ARR at reasonable penetration
- This is not a large market on its own — it is a proving ground, not an end state

National specialty crop supply chain operators (multi-state aggregators, regional distributors with local sourcing programs) represent the larger commercial opportunity.
That market is reached after Indiana pilot credibility is established.

## Why Adoption Is Hard

- farms are not ideal first customers and should not be targeted first
- food hubs often operate as nonprofits or on thin margins — budget is real but limited
- outcome cycles are seasonal: the value of pre-season planning cannot be demonstrated within a standard monthly billing period
- data is inconsistent across buyers and growers — expect messy input formats, incomplete history, and SKU normalization work in every pilot
- trust matters more than dashboards — operators adopt tools from people they already trust, not from cold software pitches
- many operators believe they already have a good-enough process — the pain must be made visible before they will pay to solve it

## Key Unvalidated Assumption

The pricing range of $5,000–$15,000 for a pilot assumes operators connect their planning pain to a measurable dollar cost.

This assumption needs active validation.

If operators treat supply mismatch as an unavoidable cost of the business rather than a solvable operational problem, willingness to pay will be lower than expected.

The discovery call objective is as much about surfacing cost consciousness as it is about demonstrating the product.

## Indiana / Midwest Implication

Indiana is a strong pilot geography but not proof of a universal category.

The initial market must stay focused on operators serving recurring specialty-crop demand, especially where buyer reliability matters.
Expanding before the wedge is proven in one geography will dilute execution focus and produce weaker validation data.

## Commercial Implication

The best first sale is a paid pilot tied to visible operator pain, not a broad platform contract.

The pilot model also serves a diagnostic function: it reveals whether the operator's data is clean enough to work with, whether their decision-making cycle allows for weekly planning input, and whether they will actually change behavior based on output.
